## A 402, three cheques, and one answer to pay for

Picture a toll booth on a road that leads to a library.

The booth charges per car. It charges the same whether the car is carrying a
researcher who will spend a year with one book, a photocopier operator who will
duplicate the whole collection, or a lost tourist who turns around at the door.
The toll is a fact about the road. It is not a fact about the library, and it is
certainly not a fact about what anyone did with a book.

That is the 402. It is a real wall, it collects real money, and the quantity it
meters is traffic. Everything difficult in this book comes from the gap between
the thing that is easy to count at the gate and the thing you actually wanted to
be paid for, which happened much later, inside someone's head, and left no
receipt.

Now the three ways of dividing up a pot, in a form with no marketplace in it at
all. Imagine a village where three people contributed to a shared meal. One grew
the vegetables. One did the cooking. One owns the pot everyone ate from.

**Split it equally.** Nobody argues, nobody is happy, and the person who grew the
vegetables for six months gets the same as the person who lent a pot for an hour.
The rule ignores everything about the meal. Its virtue is that it cannot be
gamed by working out what the rule measures, because it measures nothing.

**Split it by who got thanked at the table.** This sounds much better - it
tracks something real - until you notice who gets thanked. The cook, who was
visible at the moment of serving. The pot's owner, whose pot is sitting in the
middle of the table. The person who grew the vegetables was in a field in April
and is not at dinner. Being thanked is a fact about the seating arrangement.

That is citation counting. The retriever is the seating arrangement.

**Split it by what would have been missing.** Take each person away and ask how
much worse the meal is. This is the honest question, and it is the one that
breaks in the most interesting way: if the neighbours also grow the same
vegetables, then removing the grower changes nothing, because the vegetables were
available anyway. The measurement says the grower contributed nothing. It is
right, in a narrow and infuriating sense - the meal would have happened - and it
is exactly wrong about who did the work.

Sit with the discomfort of that third case, because it is not a flaw in a
particular formula, and no amount of better measurement fixes it. Counterfactual
questions do not reward origination. They reward *scarcity*. A source that is the
only place something exists is enormously valuable by this measure; the same
source, once copied twice, is worth nothing by the same measure, without having
changed at all.

And a last picture for the noise, which is the part that people skip. Imagine
weighing three sacks on a scale that reads correctly on average but wobbles by a
kilogram either way each time you use it. You weigh them: 0.5 kg, 1.5 kg, 3.7 kg.
You publish a pie chart. The chart looks decisive. But you could re-weigh and get
1.5, 0.9, 3.4, and the first two sacks would swap places. The pie chart has more
confidence in it than the scale does. Every attribution demo you will ever see
has this problem, and almost none of them mention the scale.

## The gatekeeper

Think of a walled medieval town that grew up around the only bridge.

At first the wall is just a wall - it keeps people out. Then someone realizes
that everyone entering has to be identified, so the town issues seals. Then, since
everyone at the gate already has a seal, the town starts posting rules about what
visitors may do inside. Then it collects the tolls itself rather than letting each
household collect at its door. Then it publishes a directory of which households
have what to sell. And then it buys the market square.

At no point did anything sinister happen. Each step is a reasonable convenience
built on the previous step, several of them are genuinely open - anyone may read
the rules, anyone may mint a seal - and the town has done more for the households
than anyone else has. But look at where you stand if you want to start a rival
market. You may either rent space inside the walls, on the town's terms, or you
may set up outside, where four fifths of the countryside lives and where there is
no gate, no seal, and nothing to stop anyone taking whatever they want. Both
options are real businesses. Neither of them is competing with the town.

The other picture worth carrying from this section is the one about locking your
door.

Your recipe is in your kitchen. You lock the door. The recipe is also on a card
you gave your sister, in a church cookbook from 1998, on a food blog that copied
it, and in a photograph on someone's phone. Locking the door was worth doing. The
lock is why the restaurant chain finally called you and offered a licence. But if
you believe the lock is the reason nobody else has the recipe, you will spend your
money on better locks, and the recipe will keep turning up, and you will never
understand why.

The right thing to spend money on is finding out how many copies exist and where.
That is not a lock, it is a search, and it is a different product.

## What the deals actually buy

Two doors, and everybody keeps describing the wrong one.

Behind the first door: a lab buys a pile of text, takes it home, trains on it,
and never comes back. One payment, one transfer, done. That is a training deal,
and it is what everyone pictures when they hear "AI licensing."

Behind the second door: a lab does not want your text at all. It wants a phone
line to your newsroom that stays open, so that when a user asks about something
that happened this morning, it can call you. It pays for the line, not for the
words. That is an access deal, and it is where the traffic is now going. The first
door is not closing, but the second one is twice as busy every year.

Here is the picture that makes the shift obvious rather than surprising. Text on
the internet is not scarce. There is more of it than anyone can train on, and a
model that has read a hundred articles about a subject learns very little from the
hundred and first. What *is* scarce is (a) what happened in the last hour, which
no amount of old text contains, and (b) the judgment of someone who genuinely
knows a subject, which is not lying around on the web at all.

So the money goes to the two scarce things. Access deals buy the first. The
expert-data market - the one where a single labeling vendor out-earns the entire
content-licensing business - buys the second. If you want to be paid a lot for
data, you have to have something scarce, and "words, in general" stopped being
scarce some time ago.

One more picture, for reading deal numbers. A headline deal figure is like a
house price that includes the furniture, the car in the garage, an agreement not
to sue over the fence, and a promise that the seller will keep mowing the lawn.
Somebody paid $250 million for all of it at once. Asking "so what is the content
worth per word" is asking what the fence agreement was worth, when the contract
never itemized it and both parties preferred it that way.

## What the courts priced

Imagine a factory that has been taking water from a river to make its product.

The court's ruling, in plain shape, is: making the product is fine, and you may
keep doing it. But you climbed a fence to get to the river, and the fence-climbing
costs you $1.5 billion. Pay for the trespass.

The factory reads that judgment, pays, and then does the obvious thing: it buys a
legitimate water connection, once, and carries on making exactly the same product
forever, paying nothing further. Nothing in the ruling created a per-litre price.
It created a one-time penalty for the manner of getting in.

The reason people misread this so consistently is that the number is enormous, and
enormous numbers feel like turning points. But look at what the number is
attached to. Divide it by the works involved and it is about the price of a very
expensive book, paid once. It is not the beginning of a meter running. It is the
closing of an old account.

What could still change the picture is a different complaint entirely - not "you
took my thing" but "your product now sits where my product used to sit, and my
readers go to you." That is a claim about substitution in the market, not about
copying, and it is genuinely unresolved. If that one lands, everything reprices,
because you cannot cure substitution by buying a clean copy. This is the one
pending question in the whole legal landscape that would create a recurring
payment rather than a one-time one, and it is why the honest posture is to build
what is needed either way rather than to bet on the outcome.

## Contributive and corroborative

Two questions that sound identical and are not:

- "Where did this come from?"
- "Who says so?"

Ask a doctor how she knows a drug interacts badly with grapefruit. She might tell
you about the paper she read in residency twenty years ago - that is where it came
from, and it is a fact about her history. Or she might turn her monitor around and
show you the reference database entry she just looked up - that is who says so,
and it is a fact about what is on her screen right now.

Both are true. Both are useful. They are answers to different questions, and the
second one is much easier to produce, which is why it is the one every product
gives you.

Notice what happens if you pay based on the second answer. The reference database
gets paid every time. The paper from twenty years ago - the one that actually put
the fact in the doctor's head - is never mentioned, because it is not on the
screen. You would be paying the display, not the education.

There is a clean test, and it needs no technology. Change what is on the screen
and ask again. If the answer to your question changes, you were asking "who says
so." A fact about the doctor's education cannot change because you swapped her
monitor.

The reason this distinction is the spine of the book: everything that is easy to
do is on the "who says so" side, everything that is worth paying for is on the
"where did it come from" side, and the entire commercial vocabulary uses one word
for both.

One more thing to picture, because the "where did it come from" side is stranger
than it looks. When a model finishes training, the books are gone. Not stored
somewhere and referenced - gone. What is left is a very large collection of
settings that were nudged, a few billion times, by every sentence that went past.
Asking which book is responsible for a particular capability is not like asking
which folder a file is in. It is like asking which meal is responsible for a
particular part of your bone. The question has a real answer, in principle,
measurable by careful counterfactual experiments. It does not have a lookup.

## Three ways to pay, and what each one misallocates

The clearest picture of a pooled royalty is a restaurant with one bill.

Everyone puts $20 in the middle. At the end of the night, the total is divided
among the dishes according to how many portions of each were ordered across the
whole restaurant, all night, at every table. You had the soup. Your $20 is
distributed across the steaks, the wine, and the desserts that other tables
ordered, in proportion to the restaurant's traffic. You will pay for wine you did
not drink, and the soup you did have will earn its maker a share of the evening's
total that is unrelated to what you personally handed over.

Nobody stole anything. There is no villain. That is simply what a shared pot
does: it converts every individual's spending into a vote on a single global
ranking. And once you see it as a ranking, the rest follows immediately.

**The head of the ranking eats.** Any ranking of human attention is steep. A few
dishes get most of the orders, so a few dishes get most of the money, and the
hundredth dish gets a rounding error. This is not a consequence of the pot being
too small; doubling the pot doubles the rounding error.

**The ranking is worth attacking.** If a share of the evening's money is decided
by counting orders, then the profitable move is not to cook better food. It is to
place orders. In music this requires fake listeners, which is expensive enough to
be an arms race. In AI, the equivalent of an order is a citation, and getting
cited means being ranked highly by a retriever - which is a machine, which is
patient, which can be probed, and which has no taste. You do not need to fool a
human being. You need to be plausible to a similarity search, and plausible text
is the cheapest thing in the world now.

Which brings the section back to a single sentence worth carrying out of it:
whatever a payment scheme counts, someone will manufacture, and the cost of
manufacturing it is the real security parameter. Counting citations is cheap to
attack. Counting genuine contribution is expensive to attack, because the only way
to fake it is to actually know something the model does not.

## The strongest case against all of this

Picture two shops on the same street.

The first sells tickets to a concert that has not been scheduled. The venue is not
booked, the band has not agreed to play, and no one has ever bought a ticket to
this particular concert anywhere in the world. The shop is beautifully fitted out.
Everyone agrees the concert would be wonderful.

The second sells scales. Its customers are people who are already trading, already
arguing about weights, and already required by law to state what they are selling.
Some of them are trading in the concert's direction, and some are not, and the
scale sells either way.

That is the whole strategic argument of this unit. The eight objections in canon
are all about the concert - whether it happens, who books it, whether the band
shows up. Not one of them is about whether scales work or whether anybody needs
one. If the concert happens, the scale shop sells scales at the door, because
nobody will accept a payout they cannot check. If it never happens, the scale shop
still has customers.

The temptation, and it is a strong one, is that the ticket shop is more exciting.
It has a bigger number on the wall. Founders reach for it, decks are built around
it, and the whole thing rests on a concert that no promoter has yet agreed to
book. Notice that the argument for the scale shop does not require predicting
whether the concert happens. That is the point of it. Any argument you can make
without a prediction is stronger than one you cannot.

## At the bench: what counts as one source

The question "what is one source?" sounds like paperwork and is actually the
whole design.

Picture the payout table at the end of the project: a list of names, each with a
percentage next to it. Now ask, for each row, who cashes the cheque. If the row
says "paper #40118," nobody cashes it. There is no such person. If the row says
"a journal," someone can cash it, but that someone is a publisher representing
thousands of authors who will then have their own argument about the split - an
argument you have made no easier, and possibly harder, by having measured at the
wrong level.

The rule that resolves it: a source is whoever could sign the contract. Work
backwards from the cheque, not forwards from the file system. Files, shards, and
documents are how the data is arranged on disk, and the arrangement on disk has no
opinion about who is owed money.

Two more pictures for the two constraints that follow.

**Why the name must be stable.** Imagine an accountant who renames the categories
in the ledger every quarter. Each quarter's books are internally consistent and no
two quarters can be compared. If a source's identity is derived from the pipeline
- a hash of the file layout, a directory name that changed when you reorganized -
then March's contribution number and June's are numbers about different things,
and the first question any auditor asks is one you cannot answer.

**Why the labels go on before the deduplication.** Two crates of apples arrive
from two farms. You tip them into one bin, because apples are apples and you only
need to count them once. Later, someone asks which farm's apples went into the
pie. There is no answer. Not a hard-to-find answer - there is no answer, because
the information was destroyed at the moment of tipping, casually, for a good
reason, by someone who was not thinking about money.

Deduplication is that moment. It happens millions of times, silently, for
excellent engineering reasons, and every time it happens a decision is made about
who gets credit for a shared passage. Recording the cluster - which sources had
this passage before you kept one copy - is what keeps the answer available. It
costs almost nothing at ingestion time and cannot be recovered afterwards at any
price.
